Black Hat

Write-up

This is a parallel coordinates chart where each line is one racial group (Black, White, Hispanic/Latino, Asian, Native American and Pacific Islander). Each city (New York, D.C., Baltimore, Atlanta and Oakland) gets two axes: Gentrified True and Gentrified False. Where a line crosses an axis shows that group's average share of residents in those neighborhoods. Behind the averages, every one of the 2,750 neighborhoods draws a faint line per group, from its own value across to the other group's average in its city. A switch flips between 2000 and 2017 and animates the lines, and hovering a line shows its exact values. On purpose, the chart says the makeup of these neighborhoods held steady. It didn't. In D.C.'s gentrified neighborhoods, the average Black share fell from 84.2% to 57.4%, and in Atlanta's from 86.7% to 63.1%.

Encoding (the main trick): each axis has its own scale, running from 0 to the largest value on that axis in the year shown, so whichever group is largest is always pinned near the top. When you flip to 2017, the D.C. True axis shrinks from 90% to 60%, so the Black line actually moves up, from 94% to 96% of the axis, even though the share dropped almost 27 points. In Atlanta it also rises, from 96% to 97%. Readers compare the shape of the lines, not the tick labels, and the shape barely changes. Because the axes don't share a scale, the True and False axes in the same city can't be compared by eye either.

Data (who's included): each False axis averages every other neighborhood in the city, including wealthy ones that could never gentrify, instead of the low-income neighborhoods that were eligible but didn't. In D.C. those 44 eligible neighborhoods were still 87.1% Black in 2017, but the chart's False group has only 45.8%, so gentrified neighborhoods seem to just be moving toward "normal." The averages also count each neighborhood equally instead of weighting by population, and the source line just says "2,750 census tracts," which sounds complete.

Title and labels: "Neighborhood Racial Makeup Has Held Steady Since 2000" tells readers what to see before they look, and "Flip between years to see for yourself" makes the switch feel like proof. Readers flip it, see the same shape, and trust the claim more because they "checked" it.

Every number is real, the hover box shows them, and the faint lines make it look like nothing is hidden. But the faint lines use the same stretched scales, and any neighborhood above the top of an axis is simply cut off, so they hide the change just as well. To see the real story, put every axis on the same 0–100% scale and compare gentrified neighborhoods only to other eligible ones, like our white hat chart does.
