Assignment 2: White Hat / Black Hat

Gentrification and demographic change in five U.S. cities, 2000 to 2017

Mihail Gjoni, Tong Zhan

Data question: In which of five U.S. cities did gentrification change the racial makeup of neighborhoods the most, from 2000 to 2017?

White Hat

How to read this chart

  • Each section represents one city, showing the 4 primary racial groups (Black, White, Hispanic / Latino, and Asian).
  • Each group displays a pair of horizontal bars: solid, vibrant bars = lower-income neighborhoods that gentrified; faded, outlined bars = similar eligible neighborhoods that did not gentrify. n is the count of neighborhoods.
  • In Change (2017 − 2000) mode, bars extend left from the center line for net losses and right for net gains (in percentage points).
  • In 2017 Share and 2000 Share modes, bars show population-weighted demographic shares from 0% to 100%.
  • Use the Filter City buttons to focus on a single city or compare all five at once. Hover any bar for exact headcounts and baseline shares.

Write-up

Our goal with the white hat visualization was to see how neighborhood racial demographics actually moved when low-income areas gentrified. To do this, we pulled census numbers that BuzzFeed News collected across DC, Atlanta, Oakland, Baltimore, and New York. Governing magazine defines an area as eligible to gentrify if its 2000 median income/home values sat in the bottom 40% metro-wide. Whenever a neighborhood also jumped into the top third for home values/college degrees by 2017, we labeled it gentrified.

We filtered the data down to just the 882 neighborhoods that met those starting requirements (289 gentrified and 593 that didn't). That way we avoided lumping in rich suburbs that couldn't gentrify to begin with. We also weighted each neighborhood by population so larger areas with 8,000 residents wouldn't get treated like tiny 800-person tracts. Then we computed the net demographic shift for each racial group between 2000/2017 shares.

The old parallel coordinates chart had lines crossing between completely separate cities, making it look like residents were migrating from DC to Atlanta. That didn't make much visual/logical sense for categorical data. So we swapped it out for a grouped horizontal diverging bar chart. Diverging bars was way simpler and clearer to read: gains stretch out to the right from zero, and losses stretch out to the left. Each city pairs up gentrified areas (solid bars) directly against similiar non-gentrified areas (lighter bars). We slapped the exact shift right onto each bar so viewers didn't have to guess and squint at tiny axes. Toggle buttons up top make it easy to flip back and forth between net change and raw 2000/2017 demographic shares.

Across each city, gentrified neighborhoods dropped lots of Black residents while picking up White residents. Non-gentrified areas barely budged. DC saw the biggest swing: gentrified neighborhoods lost 25.5 percentage points of Black residents (falling from 82.2% down to 56.8%), while non-gentrified tracts only dipped 4.0 points. White residents in those gentrified DC tracts climbed 18.8 points over the exact same period. Atlanta looked similiar, dropping 22.5 points of Black residents in gentrified neighborhoods while non-gentrified areas barely budged (down 1.2 points).

One caveat is that 2017 numbers come from five-year ACS samples, meaning smaller populations/neighborhoods carry bigger margins of error. Also, this only captures net neighborhood counts over seventeen years rather than tracking individual families who moved away.

Black Hat

How to read this chart

  • Each bold line is one race. Where it crosses an axis is that race's average share of residents.
  • Each city has two axes: True = neighborhoods that gentrified, False = the city's other neighborhoods.
  • Each faint line is one neighborhood. It starts at that neighborhood's real share and runs to the other group's average.
  • Flip the switch to compare 2000 and 2017, and hover a line for exact numbers.

Write-up

For the black hat chart, our goal was convincing viewers that neighborhood demographics barely shifted between 2000 and 2017. It's built as an interactive parallel coordinates chart with lines tracing racial averages across alternating gentrified and non-gentrified axes. We threw in an animated Play toggle so people can flip between 2000 Census/2017 ACS data. Even with huge demographic turnover in DC and Atlanta, misleading axes and a confident title make everything look completely unchanged.

Our main method was messing with the axis scales/ranges. Instead of locking every axis to 0-100%, each vertical axis rescales to the highest number for that group in that year. So when you switch from 2000 to 2017, the top of the DC gentrified axis compresses from 90% down to 60%. Because that axis shrinks so much, the Black resident line creeps upward on the screen (from 94% to 96% height) even though actual Black share dropped from 84.2% down to 57.4%. Atlanta does the exact same thing, where an 86.7% to 63.1% plunge ends up looking like a flat/slightly rising line. Viewers instantly read line slopes/shapes before squinting at tiny labels, so the visual impression overrides the real data.

Secondly, we manipulated the baseline comparison group. Instead of comparing gentrified areas against similiar low-income neighborhoods that didn't gentrify, the non-gentrified axes lump in every other neighborhood in the city. This pulled in wealthy historically White suburbs that were never eligible to gentrify in the first place. In DC, low-income tracts that didn't gentrify stayed 87.1% Black in 2017. Mixing in the rest of the city dragged that baseline down to 45.8%, making gentrified areas look like they were just moving toward a normal citywide average.

Finally, we added visual noise and biased labeling to hide the real data. Behind the bold lines, thousands of faint background strands link individual neighborhoods/groups to opposite averages, creating visual noise that hides the real local variation. Titling the chart "Neighborhood Racial Makeup Has Held Steady Since 2000" and telling people to "Flip between years to see for yourself" primed viewers to believe the graphic before checking the actual numbers.

Development Process

We divided the workload between coding the visualization skeleton and refining the charts/write-ups. Mihail spent roughly 5.5 hours setting up the base HTML/CSS skeleton and getting the first working versions of both charts on the page. He ported the parallel coordinates setup over from syntagmatic's D3 v4 code to D3 v7, which included wiring up the SVG race lines alongside the faint canvas background strands. Tong spent around 6 hours overhauling the white hat visualization and completing the write-ups. The biggest task was redesigning the white hat chart: parallel coordinates felt confusing for comparing city groups, so Tong replaced it with a grouped horizontal diverging bar chart for visual/logical clarity. Tong also wrote the detailed explanations for both visualizations, documenting the Governing criteria, data filtering rules, and deceptive axis methods.

Adjusting the axis transitions with D3 took Mihail the longest, especially synchronizing the canvas paths so faint lines rendered smoothly without lagging during year flips. For Tong, getting the population-weighted demographic shifts calculated and aligning the diverging bar scales/labels cleanly took the most time. All together, we logged roughly 12 hours between the two of us to get everything finished.

AI Usage

We each used a different AI assistant to speed up code setup and D3 syntax checks. Mihail used Claude to help port syntagmatic's D3 v4 parallel coordinates code into D3 v7. Claude was mainly helpful for setting up the initial HTML/CSS grid layout and generating the canvas rendering loop for the faint background lines. Mihail also consulted Claude to test deceptive axis options for the black hat chart, such as dropping coordinate tick marks and stretching axis bounds.

Tong used Gemini while designing the white hat diverging bar chart and drafting the project write-ups. Gemini helped brainstorm the D3 horizontal scale logic and compute population-weighted demographic shifts across city tract groups. However, Gemini regularly produced similiar positioning bugs where labels overlapped negative bars or text truncated unexpectedly, which required hands-on debugging in the live server. In short, AI worked great as a fast reference for tricky D3 methods, but all the actual visual/logical decisions and data verification were finished by us.
