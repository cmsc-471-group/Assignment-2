White Hat

How to read this chart

  • Each bold line is one race. Its height on an axis is that race's share of residents (weighted by population).
  • Each city has two axes: True = lower-income neighborhoods that gentrified, False = similar ones that didn't. n is the number of neighborhoods.
  • Every axis uses the same scale, so you can compare heights anywhere on the chart.

  • Pick 2000, 2017 (2000 shown dashed), or Change to see the gain or loss in percentage points. Hover a line for exact numbers.

Write-up

Question and data: in which of five U.S. cities did gentrification change the racial makeup of neighborhoods the most, from 2000 to 2017? To answer it fairly, each city's gentrified neighborhoods are compared with similar ones that didn't gentrify. We use BuzzFeed News's census tract data for New York, D.C., Baltimore, Atlanta and Oakland, which compares the 2000 Census with the 2013–2017 American Community Survey. Following Governing magazine's method, BuzzFeed calls a tract eligible if in 2000 it had at least 500 residents and its median income and home value were in the bottom 40% of its metro area. It's gentrified if it was eligible and then ranked in the top third of its metro for growth in both bachelor's degrees and inflation-adjusted home value.

Data transformations: we keep only the 882 eligible tracts (289 gentrified, 593 not), so every True/False pair compares neighborhoods that started out alike. Averages are weighted by population (total people of a race divided by total people), so a tract of 8,000 people counts more than a tract of 800. We also calculate the change for each group, 2017 share minus 2000 share, in percentage points.

Marks and encodings: each race is one colored line, and each city gets a True and a False axis. Every axis shares one 0–100% scale with light gridlines, so a height means the same thing everywhere on the chart. Bold lines are the averages, and faint dots are single neighborhoods at their real values, so readers can see the spread behind each average. The 2017 view keeps 2000 as a dashed line, and the Change view puts 0 in the middle, so gains and losses can be read directly instead of compared across two pictures. Hovering shows the exact numbers for every city. Colors stay the same in every view, and nothing is cut off.

Titles and labels: the title is neutral, and the subtitle states the finding: in all five cities, the Black share fell more in gentrified neighborhoods than in similar ones that didn't, while the White share rose. In D.C. it fell about 25 points (82.2% to 56.8%) versus 4 points; in Atlanta, 23 points versus 1. Every axis shows its sample size (n), and the notes say the averages are population-weighted and that Hispanic/Latino can be of any race, so shares can add to more than 100%.

Limitations and bias: the 2017 numbers are survey estimates with margins of error, which are larger for small tracts and small groups, and some city/status groups are small (Atlanta True has 23 tracts). "Gentrified" follows BuzzFeed's definition, and other definitions would sort some tracts differently. Finally, the chart shows who lives in a neighborhood, not who moved out, so it doesn't measure displacement directly.

Black Hat

How to read this chart

  • Each bold line is one race. Where it crosses an axis is that race's average share of residents.
  • Each city has two axes: True = neighborhoods that gentrified, False = the city's other neighborhoods.
  • Each faint line is one neighborhood. It starts at that neighborhood's real share and runs to the other group's average.
  • Flip the switch to compare 2000 and 2017, and hover a line for exact numbers.

Write-up

This is a parallel coordinates chart where each line is one racial group (Black, White, Hispanic/Latino, Asian, Native American and Pacific Islander). Each city (New York, D.C., Baltimore, Atlanta and Oakland) gets two axes: Gentrified True and Gentrified False. Where a line crosses an axis shows that group's average share of residents in those neighborhoods. Behind the averages, every one of the 2,750 neighborhoods draws a faint line per group, from its own value across to the other group's average in its city. A switch flips between 2000 and 2017 and animates the lines, and hovering a line shows its exact values. On purpose, the chart says the makeup of these neighborhoods held steady. It didn't. In D.C.'s gentrified neighborhoods, the average Black share fell from 84.2% to 57.4%, and in Atlanta's from 86.7% to 63.1%.

Encoding (the main trick): each axis has its own scale, running from 0 to the largest value on that axis in the year shown, so whichever group is largest is always pinned near the top. When you flip to 2017, the D.C. True axis shrinks from 90% to 60%, so the Black line actually moves up, from 94% to 96% of the axis, even though the share dropped almost 27 points. In Atlanta it also rises, from 96% to 97%. Readers compare the shape of the lines, not the tick labels, and the shape barely changes.

Data (who's included): each False axis averages every other neighborhood in the city, including wealthy ones that could never gentrify, instead of the low-income neighborhoods that were eligible but didn't. In D.C. those 44 eligible neighborhoods were still 87.1% Black in 2017, but the chart's False group has only 45.8%, so gentrified neighborhoods seem to just be moving toward "normal."

Title and labels: "Neighborhood Racial Makeup Has Held Steady Since 2000" tells readers what to see before they look, and "Flip between years to see for yourself" makes the switch feel like proof.

Why it's a black hat

  • Separate scales: each axis stretches to its own max, so the largest group stays near the top. In D.C. True, Black share fell 84.2% → 57.4%, but the line moves up.
  • Wrong comparison: "False" includes wealthy neighborhoods that could never gentrify, so gentrified ones look like they're just becoming "normal."
  • Cut-off lines: neighborhoods above an axis's top are chopped off, which hides the biggest changes.
  • Fake evidence: faint lines end at an average, not real data. They look thorough but add nothing.
  • Slanted title: "Held Steady" and "see for yourself" tell readers what to conclude.

What would make it a white hat

  • Put every axis on the same 0–100% scale.
  • Compare gentrified neighborhoods only to eligible ones that didn't gentrify.
  • Show the change directly (2017 minus 2000) instead of making readers compare two pictures.
  • Show every neighborhood with nothing cut off, and weight averages by population.
  • Use a neutral title, like "How Neighborhood Racial Makeup Changed, 2000–2017."
